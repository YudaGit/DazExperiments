**Purpose:** Manuscript argument, intended structure, and current evidence map. Analyses remain exploratory; detailed process-model comparisons from Study 2 currently concern YL alone. Statements identified as plans or author interpretations below are not yet verified results.

## Central argument

Repeated colours yield a robust continuous-report advantage, but its source cannot be located by exposure duration alone. Study 1 asks how redundancy and viewing time change the *whole signed-error distribution* in a fixed six-item context. Study 2 asks whether an account of those changes also explains set size and array context. The online experiments address generality, but their data and final inclusion have not yet been audited. The manuscript should distinguish observed distributional effects, descriptive mixture components, and explanatory mechanisms at every step.

The present evidence does **not** establish that redundancy gain is post-encoding or that longer exposure leaves target quality unchanged. A faster encoding-rate account remains viable without quantitative time-course predictions and a manipulation of processing after the mask. The strongest current question is: **What joint process can explain redundancy gain, duration-related changes in central and broad errors, and array-context effects?**

**Manuscript naming:** Study 1 in the analysis files is manuscript **Experiment 1**; the three online studies are **Experiments 2a–2c**; Study 2 in the analysis files is manuscript **Experiment 3**. This gives five experiments in total. The online studies precede Experiment 3 in the intended narrative even though the analysis folders are named by laboratory study. Keep these labels distinct when transferring results into the manuscript.

## Study 1 evidence to carry forward

The [active notebook](Enc_smallN_1st/AnalysesR/s1_smallN_analysis_discrete.Rmd) and its output tables are the source for statistics; the [older pipeline note](Enc_smallN_1st/AnalysesR/legacy/WORK_NOTE_discrete_pipeline.md) records implementation history and some superseded interpretations.

- Describe circular SD, response time, and signed-error shape first. R circular SD is lower than NR in all 28 participant-by-duration comparisons. The 50-to-350 ms NR circular-SD change ranges from about −2° to −22° across participants. A small change in a single summary can coexist with a change in the distribution's peak or tails.
- The author's initial reading of the R-minus-NR circular-SD contrast is that its magnitude changes little and nonmonotonically across the seven durations. This is a **descriptive contrast on the circular-SD scale**, not an equivalence test or proof of a duration-independent mechanism; lower R circular SD corresponds to a negative R-minus-NR value. Do not transfer the group-level or plotted impression of nearly flat curves to every participant or to the full error distribution.
- The signed-error ladder compares target-only (L0), target plus uniform residual (L1), and a model adding non-target-colour responses (L2). L1 and L2 predict held-out trials better than L0, although several L0 leave-one-out diagnostics need review. L2 improves trial-level predictive performance over L1 for AQ, HC, and YIL; YDL numerically favours L1. These are within-participant, within-session trial predictions, not generalisation to new people.
- In L1, freeing target concentration across the 14 cue-by-duration cells yields no clear, consistent predictive gain over two cue-specific concentrations. The 14κ-minus-2κ paired ELPD differences are AQ −1.5 ± 5.9, HC +2.9 ± 6.5, YIL +13.9 ± 8.9, and YDL −3.2 ± 6.6. The compact 2κ fit is a descriptive baseline, **not** evidence that target precision is constant. Under its fixed-κ restriction, duration differences must largely enter the uniform weight. When κ is free, some duration contrasts move into target concentration. Do not call the uniform component a measured guess rate.
- A separate, session-pooled, single-von-Mises BIC comparison favours 1NR+7Rκ for all four people. That likelihood folds broad errors into κ and cannot adjudicate mixture components or processing stage. Keep it as a diagnostic, not the manuscript's primary model choice.
- Before final reporting, review L0 diagnostics, full-duration posterior predictions, participant-level contrasts, and session sensitivity. The [legacy notebook](Enc_smallN_1st/AnalysesR/legacy/s1_smallN_analysis_discrete_full_2026-09-29.Rmd) retains prior regressions and analyses.

## Study 2 modelling status and interpretation

### Reading for perceptual calibration and the report stage

- [Schurgin, Wixted, & Brady (2020), *Psychophysical scaling reveals a unified theory of visual memory strength*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-00938-0). Measure colour similarity separately from memory performance (Fig. 1d/f) and visible-colour matching errors (Fig. 1e). Their TCC model uses similarity to set the mean match signal and matching errors to constrain correlations between nearby signals; neither measurement is itself the full memory-error distribution. The visible-matching task is a precedent for our calibration, but their choice mechanism differs from our direct Gaussian mapping kernel.
- [Tomić & Bays (2024), *Perceptual similarity judgments do not predict the distribution of errors in working memory*](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001172). Direct test of transferring perceptual similarity judgments to memory-error predictions. Read as a challenge to assuming one task's similarity function is automatically the memory representation.
- [Tomić, Adamcová, Fehér, & Bays (2024), *Dissecting the components of error in analogue report tasks*](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-024-02453-w). Compares visible and delayed colour reproduction, wheel properties, and forced choice. Relevant to what visible matching includes beyond motor noise, and to matching the calibration apparatus and response procedure to Study 2.
- [Ngiam & Lee (2025 preprint), *Similarity judgments and visual working memory do not share the same cognitive representation*](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/fm9vz_v1). Generative reanalysis of perceptual reproduction, memory reproduction, and similarity comparison from Tomić and Bays. The inferred representation differs between similarity judgment and reproduction tasks. Treat as a preprint and assess its model assumptions before using its conclusion; [code and task-specific notebooks](https://github.com/PALM-Lab/colorModeling) are available.

**Implication for our proposed session:** Prioritise visible-colour matching with the same OKLab stimuli, wheel, and response mechanics to estimate each participant's conditional report-error width independently of Study 2 memory responses. The sample colour remains visible through response, avoiding the intended memory delay. The current candidate fit estimates an approximately 15° Gaussian mapping width freely for YL; this is **our fitted value, not a number established by Schurgin et al.** Schurgin et al.'s illustrative 2° Gaussian term concerns motor error, while their visible matching task measures broader perceptual confusability. Test whether each participant's calibration width predicts Study 2 errors when fixed or tightly constrained (with calibration uncertainty propagated). This width includes perceptual matching and wheel-response contributions, so calling it purely motor or purely decision noise would overstate the measurement. A separate similarity-judgment task would address a different quantity; its function should not be equated with the Gaussian report kernel or the latent representation without a cross-task test. Single-item masked report is a useful transfer condition, but its errors combine memory and report variability.

**Candidate participant-specific colour geometry (16 October 2026):** Similarity judgments may reveal non-uniformity around our OKLab wheel, including target-specific and clockwise/anticlockwise differences. Ngiam and Lee's key caution is that such non-uniformity need not be *shared* by similarity judgments and reproduction. Treat the measured geometry as an independently constrained candidate, not a known memory coordinate system. The current cosine/Luce and direct-Gaussian report kernels depend only on angular difference from latent `z`, so their predictions are rotation-invariant. A geometry-aware report rule could instead predict errors conditional on the actual target hue and response hue. Define explicitly whether geometry changes encoding of the target, dispersion of consolidated `z`, similarity used in mapping `z` to wheel options, or more than one stage; these assignments make different predictions. Do not silently replace `cos(response − z)` with a warped angle and interpret the fitted change as a report mechanism. A non-uniform kernel also invalidates the present single circular-convolution shortcut, which assumes the same report-error shape at every `z`.

**Proposed test sequence:** (1) Measure visible matching error and similarity judgments separately for each participant on the *same* OKLab wheel, estimating uncertainty and checking repeatability of any non-uniformity. (2) Fit competing, prespecified versions: uniform cosine, calibrated non-uniform similarity at report, calibrated visible-matching kernel, and—only if justified—a representation-space warp. Keep the direct Gaussian version as a comparator because it currently fits nearly as well as cosine/Luce and is the clearer mapping hypothesis. (3) Export actual target hues, non-target hues, item positions, wheel rotation, and signed response from the Study 2 raw trial files; the current `YL_model_trials.csv` has condition and error but omits the colour array. Compute **trial-level likelihoods and predictions** conditional on these stimuli, then evaluate held-out sessions or hues and full signed-error distributions, not just pooled cell fits. (4) Generate replicated datasets using the actual arrays as inputs and check predicted hue-dependent bias, spread, tails, and non-target attraction against observations. Simulating exact arrays alone adds no array-specific mechanism: the present AWSS fit uses unique-colour count and repetition but not the identities or distances of non-target colours. Add explicit competition or swap terms only with recoverability checks and held-out predictive benefit. A better fit after adding a flexible grid is insufficient for a process claim; the independently measured geometry must make successful, distinctive predictions without retuning it to memory errors.

The initial Bayesian target-plus-uniform fit in the [Study 2 notebook](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/s2_smallN_analysis.Rmd) gives a useful descriptive benchmark: for YL, 50-to-250 ms target-concentration contrasts are clearer than target-weight contrasts in most contexts. Four-item mixed arrays do not show the simple predicted target-weight cost to unique colours. This is one participant and one likelihood; inspect the complete signed-error predictions before mechanistic use.

More recent YL explanatory work compares a latent consolidated-angle Cauchy model with a direct MaxGumbel choice model, then imposes attended/unattended subset, set-size, redundancy-quality, and selection-bias constraints. Read the [unconstrained comparison](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/cauchy_maxgumbel_comparison_YL.md), [AWSS comparison](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/awss_comparison_YL.md), [selection extension](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/awss_selection_comparison_YL.md), and [shared-quality restriction](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/awss_shared_quality_YL.md) for definitions, cell diagnostics, and fit tables.

The selection extension materially improves both AWSS likelihoods. With duration-specific quality and selection, MaxGumbel has lower in-sample negative log likelihood than Cauchy (4680.824 versus 4706.098; 16 parameters each). With quality shared over duration, MaxGumbel again fits better (4696.425 versus 4710.289; 15 parameters each). Sharing quality costs fit, particularly for MaxGumbel, so the fitted duration effect should not be described as subset membership alone. These comparisons are in-sample and do not identify a unique cognitive mechanism. Later sensitivity files in `Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/results/YL/` compare cosine, exponential, and Gaussian decision forms; they are useful model checks, not additional participant replications. The direct Gaussian and cosine decision fits are close (negative log likelihood 4711.270 versus 4710.289) under the shared-quality Cauchy variant.

In the current AWSS parameterisation, `ColorN` is **unique-colour count**, not physical set size. The Cauchy branch scales single-colour dispersion by `sqrt(ColorN/r)`; the MaxGumbel branch scales signal amplitude by `sqrt(r/ColorN)`, with `r` the number of same-colour repetitions. `W` is a better-state probability, and `K_n = ColorN × W` is an **expected** display-level better-state count under equal selection, not a literal fixed slot cap. The [selection extension](Enc_smallN_2nd/AnalysesR/awss_selection_comparison_YL.md) derives `W_R = W + (N − 1)δ` and `W_NR = W − δ`, preserving the display average while favouring R. Relative to quality-only models, this lowers YL in-sample negative log likelihood by 123.60 for Cauchy and 60.00 for MaxGumbel. Parameter recovery, held-out prediction, and other participants remain necessary before interpreting the offset as a uniquely identified attention mechanism.

**Next discriminating work:** check observed versus predicted signed-error curves, especially far errors and the cells with largest likelihood loss; evaluate recovery and identifiability; compare appropriate held-out predictions; fit AQ when its analysis plan is fixed; and specify predictions that separate encoding rate, stochastic selection, variable precision, consolidation, and decision readout. A duration manipulation with a fixed post-mask interval cannot by itself separate these stages.

## Manuscript argument and section plan

**Narrative spine:** The prior redundancy paper establishes that repeated colours can form a common representational unit, that unique-colour count drives load, and that redundancy provides a gain beyond capacity repartitioning. This manuscript asks whether a simple redundancy-specific increase in perceptual encoding rate explains that gain, and what process model can jointly explain redundancy, duration, and array-context patterns. Experiment 1 poses the puzzle; Experiments 2a–2c check its generality and motivate better controlled data; Experiment 3 provides the main process-model test. Do not announce a post-encoding result in the opening.

The percentages below express **relative narrative emphasis**, not a fixed word budget; shared methods, figures, and technical appendices will need space in addition to these rough targets.

**Opening and focused literature review (about 25% of main text).** Introduce representational redundancy gain in delayed continuous report and distinguish it from faster detection/response in perceptual redundant-signal tasks. Then cover only VWM concepts needed for the argument: slots versus resources, continuous-report error and circular SD, neural population coding and target confusability, attention-weighted sample size, and the distinction between memory and decision/report variability. Follow with the prior paper's findings and competing mechanisms: (a) separate-channel statistical facilitation/OR selection, chiefly a response-time or detection baseline; (b) common-code coactivation and a possible faster encoding rate; (c) multiple noisy samples and attention-weighted allocation, which can change representational quality; and (d) report/mapping contributions. Briefly introduce Zhang and Luck (2008), Bays et al. (2009, 2011), TCC, CDM, and the Cauchy idea only for the specific measurement or prediction each contributes; reserve full equations and fitting details for the relevant experiment. A unitary representation alone does not prove coactivation, and coactivation alone does not entail a faster encoding rate.

**General method and analysis logic.** Define R, NR, baseline, physical set size, unique-colour count, number of repetitions, signed circular error, and circular SD before using them in results. Explain descriptive error summaries versus mixture and process parameters. Working timing description from the author: display is immediately masked; a common 750-ms postdisplay interval begins with masking and is followed by response. Check mask duration and precise timing against task code before final wording. Equal postdisplay time does not ensure equal consolidation of inputs formed under different exposures.

**Experiment 1: fixed-context puzzle (about 15%).** Report four trained participants, seven durations, R/NR contrasts, participant-specific circular-SD and signed-error patterns, and the active Bayesian mixture ladder. The author's initial 2κ target-plus-uniform interpretation is consistent with duration changes in broad/guess-like responses while represented-item variability is constrained, but the active comparison also supports non-target responses for several participants and does not establish that target variability is duration-invariant. Explain why one fixed array context and no unique-colour-load manipulation leave the mechanism unresolved. Consider Donkin et al. (2016), *Resources masquerading as slots*, for the predictable-versus-varying set-size rationale; the earlier 2013 Donkin paper is different.

**Experiments 2a–2c: online checks (about 10%).** Present 2a as an online replication of Experiment 1; 2b as the 2/4/6 set-size and 50/250-ms extension; 2c as a similar structure with Bays-style orientation-bar stimuli. The author reports that duration effects on circular SD, particularly for NR, are clearer than in Experiment 1, but the online datasets are noisy and exclusions are high. Audit sample flow, predefined exclusions, descriptive estimates, and condition structure. Their main narrative function is to motivate the trained intensive Experiment 3, not to carry the decisive mechanistic claim. Decide main-text versus supplement placement after audit.

**Experiment 3 and process model (about 35%).** Explain the close replication of 2b with trained laboratory participants and many trials per person. Show descriptive R/NR, unique-colour-load, and duration effects before model details. Introduce the planned per-person visible-colour mapping session as an external constraint on the report kernel; it has not yet been run. Model latent representational variability with Cauchy or competing MaxGumbel formulations, two better/worse quality states, sample-size/repetition scaling, duration-related quality and membership, and redundancy-related better-state selection. Compare quality-only with selection-offset models, report full signed-error predictions and residuals, and distinguish in-sample fit from predictive identification. The primary existing detailed fit is YL; do not generalise its parameter estimates to the planned sample. Inspect whether duration changes state quality as well as membership rather than assuming a pure-capacity effect.

**General discussion (about 15%).** Separate observations, model-dependent decompositions, and proposed process stages. The intended conclusion is stronger than “redundancy speeds encoding”: the combined pattern calls for representational quality and allocation mechanisms, with a report contribution constrained independently if calibration succeeds. It is weaker than proof that the gain arises during retention or consolidation. Fixed time after masking and the visible-mapping calibration do not by themselves separate perceptual encoding from later consolidation. State limits of intensive small-N inference and online exclusions, and propose a post-mask interruption, interval, or cue manipulation as a later-stage test.

**Measurement-scale caution:** The prior paper's parameter-free `sqrt(r)` rule was stated for an Oberauer-style MaxGumbel signal-amplitude parameter. The same qualitative direction may appear in circular SD or inverse circular SD, but neither is guaranteed to have exactly the same numerical scaling. Avoid calling the descriptive circular-SD gain an exact `sqrt(r)` law without a measurement-model derivation.
